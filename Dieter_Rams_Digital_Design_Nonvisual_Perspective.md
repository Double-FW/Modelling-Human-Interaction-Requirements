# Dieter Rams’s Ten Principles Reimagined for Digital Products and Services

## A first-person nonvisual perspective

Dieter Rams developed his ten principles in relation to physical products. I am applying their underlying questions to digital products and services that I experience without sight.

I have never seen a web page or app screen. I do not encounter an interface as an arrangement of boxes, colours, icons or positions. I encounter it through speech, Braille, touch, physical keys and a changing sequence of named objects, information, states and actions.

I can understand space, relationships, hierarchy and structure, but I do not share the visual memory on which many interface conventions depend. “The button on the right,” “the red area,” “the card below” or “the line moving upwards” describes a visual composition rather than giving me the meaning or action I need.

I do not speak for every blind person. People use different combinations of speech, Braille, touch, keyboards and voice input, with different experience and preferences. This first-person account represents one deliberately nonvisual way of testing Rams’s ideas.

I do not treat the ten principles as equal. Usefulness, understanding and honesty determine whether I can act independently. Thoroughness determines whether the experience works beyond the ideal path. Longevity and environmental responsibility protect continued access. Innovation, aesthetic quality and restraint are valuable only when they strengthen those outcomes.

My overall test is:

> Does the design give me meaning, control and a reliable outcome without making me reconstruct a visual interface I have never experienced?

## How I compare the principles

When principles compete, I favour:

1. **Independent completion over presentation.**
2. **Meaning and state over visual equivalence.**
3. **Control and honesty over persuasive convenience.**
4. **Predictable structure over novelty.**
5. **Efficient movement over forced sequence.**
6. **Concise clarity over either silence or excessive speech.**

A quiet interface that omits the name of a control is not restrained; it is incomplete. A visual improvement that destroys the reading order is not innovation. A simplified “blind version” with fewer functions is not useful design.

---

# 1. Innovation must reduce a real barrier

I value innovation when it gives me more direct access, reduces repetition, makes a previously visual idea understandable or lets me complete a task with greater independence.

I do not value novelty merely because it uses new technology. A gesture I cannot discover, a conversational system that hides available choices or an automatic description that invents detail can leave me with less control than a conventional interaction.

Innovation has to improve the outcome, not merely change the method. I ask:

- Can I now complete a task that was previously blocked?
- Can I reach the information with less forced listening?
- Does the innovation preserve accuracy and privacy?
- Can I understand and reverse what it does?
- Does it work with the tools and habits I already use?

I should not be made dependent on a special add-on, separate mode or experimental assistant when the ordinary service could communicate its structure and controls directly.

The outcome I want is new capability without new dependence.

---

# 2. Usefulness means independent completion

A digital service is useful to me when I can achieve the same core outcome as another user: buy, apply, learn, decide, create, communicate, consent or refuse.

The existence of spoken content does not make the service useful. I need to be able to locate the task, understand the choices, enter information, review it, act and confirm the result.

Usefulness also includes privacy. If I need a sighted person to identify an unnamed control, read a result, check a total or explain an error, the service has made assistance part of the journey. That may expose information I should be able to keep private.

I judge usefulness by asking:

- Can I complete the task from beginning to end?
- Can I do it without another person interpreting the interface?
- Can I verify what I entered and what the service recorded?
- Can I recognise and correct a mistake?
- Do I receive the same choices, information and result?

The outcome I want is agency, not merely technical access to fragments of the service.

---

# 3. Aesthetic quality exists beyond appearance

I do not experience colour, typography, spacing or visual composition. That does not mean aesthetics are absent from my interaction.

I experience aesthetic quality through:

- the rhythm and order of information;
- the clarity and tone of language;
- the relationship between labels and actions;
- the balance between useful context and repetition;
- the timing of feedback;
- the consistency of sounds, haptics and behaviour; and
- the confidence created by a coherent structure.

A well-designed experience can feel calm. It introduces itself clearly, lets me move by meaningful signposts and does not speak over my current task. A poorly designed one feels noisy even if its screen looks minimal: repeated labels, irrelevant announcements, unexplained state changes and long sequences of undifferentiated content create clutter in time rather than clutter in space.

Nonvisual aesthetics are not a spoken description of the visual theme. Telling me that a screen is elegant, blue and spacious does not reproduce its aesthetic value. The equivalent is care in the experience I actually receive.

The outcome I want is coherence, rhythm and confidence without visual dependence.

---

# 4. The product should explain itself through structure and behaviour

I cannot infer purpose from size, colour, placement or resemblance. Each meaningful object needs to tell me what it is, what it does, what it relates to and what state it is in.

I need the service to answer:

- Where am I?
- What is this content or step for?
- What can I do here?
- What information belongs together?
- Which choice is current?
- What will happen if I act?
- What changed after I acted?

I form an overview by moving among titles, sections, controls, lists and other meaningful signposts. The number of signposts should reflect the actual structure. Too few leave me in an undifferentiated stream; too many create verbal noise.

Self-explanation also requires conventional behaviour. If something identifies itself as a button, field, link or selection, I expect the familiar method of operating it. An object that claims one identity but behaves like another gives me the wrong instructions.

The outcome I want is a reliable mental model formed from meaning, not reconstructed from visual position.

---

# 5. The interface should not compete with my purpose

An unobtrusive nonvisual experience lets me concentrate on my task. It does not force me through repeated navigation, announce decorative content, interrupt me with routine updates or make every visual change part of the spoken experience.

Restraint is especially important because speech unfolds over time. One unnecessary word may be trivial; the same phrase repeated across fifty controls becomes a barrier.

I value:

- direct routes to the main task;
- the ability to bypass repetition;
- concise names that remain clear out of context;
- notifications proportionate to urgency; and
- silence for content that adds no meaning.

But quietness is not the same as omission. If a price changes, an error appears, a panel opens or an action succeeds, I need that change. The design should remove noise without removing evidence.

The outcome I want is an experience that stays quiet until information affects my understanding, decision or control.

---

# 6. The service should be honest about action and consequence

I depend heavily on names, descriptions and announced states. If these are vague or misleading, I have less surrounding context with which to detect the deception.

I expect honesty about:

- what an action will do;
- whether a choice is selected by default;
- whether a charge repeats;
- what information will be shared;
- whether content was generated or inferred;
- what the system does not know;
- what remains after a process appears complete; and
- how I can reverse or cancel the decision.

Manipulation can be nonvisual. A preferred option may be encountered first, given a clearer name or made much easier to select than to refuse. Important conditions may appear after the action in my reading sequence. A cancellation route may be buried beneath dozens of unrelated controls.

I should receive consequential information before the choice it affects, not discover it by reading backwards afterwards.

The outcome I want is informed consent and refusal, not a sequence engineered to exploit momentum.

---

# 7. The experience should preserve what I have learned

I invest time learning a service’s language, structure, shortcuts and interaction patterns. That knowledge gives me speed and independence.

A redesign that changes names, sequence or behaviour can remove those benefits instantly. The visible interface may appear fresher while my established mental model becomes unreliable.

Long-lasting nonvisual design protects:

- stable names for stable concepts;
- predictable control behaviour;
- dependable routes to common tasks;
- saved choices, history and position;
- compatibility with established input and output methods; and
- access to my data if the service changes or closes.

Change is justified when it resolves a genuine problem. It should be explained, introduced coherently and avoid forcing me to relearn unrelated parts of the service.

The outcome I want is improvement that respects the knowledge I have already built.

---

# 8. Care must include the parts nobody sees

For me, many of the most important design details are literally unseen by the sighted team. The spoken name of an icon, the sequence of a dialogue, the return point after closing it and the announcement of an error may never appear in a visual design file.

These are not technical afterthoughts. They are my interface.

Thorough design considers:

- what I hear or feel when I first arrive;
- the order in which information becomes available;
- what happens when I open and close something;
- where my attention moves after an action;
- how selection and expanded states are communicated;
- how I find an error and return to it;
- whether my work survives interruption;
- how loading, empty and failure states behave; and
- whether unusual content still makes sense.

The ideal path is not enough. A service shows respect when I can recover from its failures and my own mistakes without losing work or asking for help.

The outcome I want is a complete experience, not a visual design with an improvised spoken layer.

---

# 9. Environmental responsibility includes continued access

Digital services consume energy, data, devices and attention. I experience environmental waste when a simple task requires heavy downloads, constant background activity or a device upgrade that offers me no meaningful benefit.

I expect a responsible service to:

- work efficiently on devices that remain otherwise capable;
- avoid unnecessary audio, video and computational effects;
- limit repeated downloads and background processing;
- preserve support for established technologies where practical;
- avoid creating separate versions that duplicate maintenance; and
- let me complete tasks on a poor connection without losing progress.

Device obsolescence can have a disproportionate cost. Replacing a familiar setup may also mean replacing specialist equipment, learning new commands and re-establishing a reliable working environment.

Attention is also finite. Endless feeds and repeated prompts consume time and battery while making it harder for me to judge completeness.

The outcome I want is a service that conserves material, computational and human resources while preserving independence.

---

# 10. Use as little design as the meaning allows

For me, “less, but better” cannot mean silently removing information that a sighted user still receives through appearance and position.

A sighted person may recognise an unlabelled magnifying glass as search, see that a button is unavailable or notice that a panel has opened. If the corresponding words and states are removed from my experience, the design is not minimal. It has transferred the work of interpretation to me.

I welcome less:

- repeated navigation;
- decorative description;
- duplicated object types inside labels;
- unnecessary steps;
- irrelevant notifications;
- forced listening; and
- special “blind” features that duplicate my own tools.

I need enough:

- context to understand each choice;
- structure to move directly;
- state to know what is selected or available;
- feedback to know what changed;
- warning to understand consequences; and
- recovery to correct mistakes.

Minimalism should reduce interaction cost, not reduce meaning.

The outcome I want is the shortest clear route to full understanding and control.

---

# A nonvisual interpretation of good digital design

From my nonvisual perspective, Rams’s principles lead towards an experience designed in time as well as in structure.

I receive one piece of information after another. Good design helps me decide what to hear, what to skip, where to move and how the current object relates to my task. It gives me direct access without requiring me to remember a hidden two-dimensional arrangement.

Aesthetic quality becomes coherence and rhythm. Unobtrusiveness becomes freedom from needless speech and interruption. Thoroughness means designing names, sequence, state, focus, feedback and recovery with the same care given to visual composition. Longevity means preserving learned language and interaction. “Less” means less friction and noise—not less meaning.

The deepest implication is that there is no neutral visual product with a nonvisual layer added afterwards. If my experience is left to chance, part of the product has been left undesigned.

For me, good digital design communicates through its behaviour:

- what this is;
- where I am;
- what matters;
- what I can do;
- what each choice means;
- what changed;
- whether the action succeeded; and
- how I can continue or recover.

When it does that, I am not receiving an alternative to the product. I am using the product.
