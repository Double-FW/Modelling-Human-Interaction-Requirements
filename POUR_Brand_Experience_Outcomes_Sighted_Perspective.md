# POUR Reimagined as Brand Experience Outcomes

## A first-person sighted perspective for digital brands, products and services

I experience a brand through more than its logo, colour palette or campaign. I experience it through every promise, choice, interaction and result.

Because I am sighted, visual design has a strong influence on my first impression. I notice colour, typography, imagery, movement, spacing, hierarchy and composition. These signals can help me recognise the brand, understand what matters and anticipate how an interaction will behave.

But a brand is not what the interface looks like. It is what the organisation repeatedly enables me to perceive, do, understand and trust.

The POUR principles can therefore be understood as four expected brand experience outcomes:

- **Perceivable becomes: I feel seen.**
- **Operable becomes: I feel capable.**
- **Understandable becomes: I feel confident.**
- **Robust becomes: I feel safe.**

These outcomes combine functional and emotional experience. The functional outcome is what the product enables. The emotional outcome is what that repeated experience teaches me to believe about myself and the brand.

The relationship moves in that direction. Attractive branding cannot compensate for a blocked task. Warm language cannot restore agency to an interaction I cannot control. A message that says I am valued becomes less credible when the product behaves as though I am not.

My central expectation is:

> Make the brand promise true in the interaction.

## How I compare the outcomes

The four outcomes reinforce one another, but they are not interchangeable.

1. **I must first be able to receive the meaning.** If important information is unavailable to me, the rest of the experience has no reliable foundation.
2. **I must be able to act.** Information without agency leaves me informed but powerless.
3. **I must understand the action and its consequences.** Agency without confidence can become risk.
4. **I must be able to rely on the experience.** A journey that works only under ideal conditions does not create lasting trust.

The fifth emotional idea in the source draft—**feeling valued**—is best treated as the brand-level result of all four. It is not something a brand can claim independently. I feel valued when the system consistently allows me to be seen, capable, confident and safe.

# 1. Perceivable: I feel seen

## My functional expectation

I expect to perceive the information, identity, choices, status and meaning I need.

As a sighted person, I may first recognise these through visual relationships:

- the heading that tells me where I am;
- the contrast that separates text from its background;
- the grouping that shows which label belongs to which control;
- the scale that distinguishes the main action from supporting actions;
- the imagery that establishes character and context; and
- the visible change that confirms something happened.

The brand should not make me work against its visual identity. A pale colour palette, highly stylised typeface or cinematic animation may be distinctive while making the actual service harder to perceive.

I expect the visual system to communicate meaning without making any single visual quality carry the whole message. Colour can reinforce an error, selection or status, but the meaning should still be explicit. An icon can support recognition, but I should not have to decode an unfamiliar symbol to understand a consequential action.

## My emotional expectation

I feel seen when the brand anticipates the circumstances in which I will encounter it.

That does not mean the brand literally looks at me. It means I recognise that someone considered whether I could receive the message, find the important information and understand what is being offered.

I feel unseen when:

- brand expression overwhelms the message;
- promotional imagery displaces the task I came to perform;
- information is technically present but visually obscured;
- an experience assumes ideal lighting, a large screen or perfect concentration; or
- the organisation tells me what it wants to say without considering what I need to know.

## The brand outcome

The experience says:

> You are part of the intended audience. We have made the meaning available to you.

Feeling seen creates recognition and belonging. It turns perception from a technical event into a relationship: the brand has not placed its identity between me and the information.

## Evidence I would notice

- I can identify the organisation and purpose quickly.
- The most important information has appropriate prominence.
- Visual style clarifies rather than competes.
- Images add meaning instead of substituting for it.
- Status and change are apparent without interpretation.
- The experience continues to make sense when my context, device or display changes.

# 2. Operable: I feel capable

## My functional expectation

I expect to be able to move, choose, enter information, stop, dismiss, review, submit and recover.

Because I can see the interface, I may notice many possible actions at once. That overview is useful only when the actions behave as they appear to behave.

I expect:

- interactive objects to be recognisable;
- the primary action to be distinguishable from secondary ones;
- the entire journey to remain under my control;
- motion, media and interruptions to be stoppable;
- destructive actions to be clearly differentiated;
- progress to remain available after interruption; and
- a route back when I make a wrong choice.

Brand theatre should never interfere with control. A transition may express energy, but it should not delay me. A playful gesture may create character, but it should not be the only way to act. A persuasive journey may support conversion, but it should not make refusal deliberately harder.

## My emotional expectation

I feel capable when every intended action produces a meaningful and proportionate response.

The product does not make me feel clumsy for missing a hidden control. It does not turn an error into a judgement about my competence. It does not make me fight an animation, close repeated promotions or search for the route the organisation would prefer I did not take.

I feel incapable when the interface appears to be working against me. Repeated friction changes the emotional meaning of the brand. “Empowering” becomes patronising. “Simple” becomes restrictive. “Personal” becomes controlling.

## The brand outcome

The experience says:

> You can do this. We will support your intention rather than obstruct it.

Feeling capable creates agency. I am not being carried through a funnel; I am acting within a service.

## Evidence I would notice

- I can identify and activate every necessary action.
- The result matches the action I chose.
- I can pause, stop, go back or change my mind.
- The service preserves my work wherever practical.
- Errors help me recover without shame.
- The easiest route is not reserved for the choice that benefits the brand.

# 3. Understandable: I feel confident

## My functional expectation

I expect to know where I am, what the service is asking, what each choice means and what will happen next.

Visual design can support this through hierarchy, sequence, grouping and consistent patterns. Brand language can support it through clear terminology, an appropriate tone and explanations provided at the point of need.

I expect:

- familiar concepts to retain familiar names;
- the same visual treatment to mean the same thing;
- important conditions to appear before the relevant decision;
- progress to be clear and truthful;
- labels to describe purpose rather than internal process;
- errors to explain the problem and resolution; and
- confirmation to state what happened.

A distinctive brand voice is not an excuse for ambiguity. Humour, informality and metaphor can create warmth, but they should not obscure a payment, deadline, warning, consent choice or recovery instruction.

## My emotional expectation

I feel confident when I can predict the service and verify the result.

Confidence does not require certainty about everything. A brand can say that an outcome is unknown, an estimate may change or a system has limitations. Honest uncertainty can create more confidence than artificial certainty.

I lose confidence when visual reassurance contradicts functional reality: a calming illustration beside an unexplained error, a friendly voice hiding an unexpected fee or a progress indicator that bears little relation to the remaining work.

## The brand outcome

The experience says:

> You understand where you are, what this means and what you can do next.

Feeling confident reduces doubt without removing my responsibility to decide. The brand helps me think; it does not try to think for me.

## Evidence I would notice

- I can explain the purpose of the current step.
- Choices use language I recognise.
- Consequences are available before commitment.
- The visual sequence matches the decision sequence.
- Errors are specific, constructive and located where I need them.
- Confirmation gives me enough information to trust the outcome.

# 4. Robust: I feel safe

## My functional expectation

I expect the service to continue working when my device, browser, settings, connection or circumstances change.

For a sighted person, robustness includes more than whether the screen loads. The content should remain usable when I enlarge it, change its presentation, use a small screen, rotate the device, switch input method or return after an interruption.

I also expect the journey to tolerate ordinary variation:

- longer names and translated content;
- slow or unstable connections;
- older but capable devices;
- accidental refreshes or timeouts;
- unusual combinations of choices; and
- changes introduced by future updates.

Brand consistency should not mean pixel-for-pixel rigidity. A robust identity can adapt its layout while preserving its voice, meaning and recognisable character.

## My emotional expectation

I feel safe when the service holds together under pressure.

Safety is not only protection from security threats. It is confidence that the service will not betray my expectations, lose my work, change the rules mid-journey or abandon me when something goes wrong.

I feel unsafe when an update moves critical actions without explanation, when a timeout destroys a long form or when the brand appears dependable in advertising but fragile in use.

## The brand outcome

The experience says:

> You can rely on us. The experience will continue to make sense when conditions change.

Feeling safe turns successful use into trust. One successful transaction proves only that the service worked once. Reliability teaches me that the brand is worthy of future dependence.

## Evidence I would notice

- My task survives foreseeable changes in device and context.
- The service preserves progress and provides recovery.
- Updates improve the experience without casually destroying learned behaviour.
- The brand remains recognisable without forcing one fixed presentation.
- Failure states are designed as carefully as success states.
- I know what has been saved, sent, shared or changed.

# Feeling valued: the combined brand outcome

Feeling valued is not a decorative emotional layer and it is not achieved by adding an inclusive photograph to a campaign.

I feel valued when the organisation repeatedly demonstrates that my time, attention, decisions and circumstances matter.

Functionally, that means:

- I can receive the meaning;
- I can complete the task;
- I can understand the decision;
- I can rely on the result; and
- I am not offered a reduced or neglected version of the service.

Emotionally, it means:

- I belong here;
- my effort is not treated as free;
- my mistakes do not reduce my dignity;
- my refusal is respected as much as my consent; and
- the brand’s interest in me is not limited to conversion.

Feeling valued is therefore the test of whether the four outcomes operate as a coherent brand experience.

---

# Balancing functional and emotional experience

Functional and emotional outcomes are connected, but they should not be confused.

## Function creates the evidence

I cannot be persuaded into feeling capable while the interaction prevents me from acting. I cannot be reassured into feeling safe while the service repeatedly loses my work.

The functional experience supplies the evidence on which the emotional relationship is built.

## Emotion interprets the pattern

A single delay may be frustrating. Repeated delays, obscure choices and weak recovery become a message: my time is less important than the organisation’s process.

Emotion is not an optional flourish. It is how I interpret the accumulated behaviour of the service.

## Brand expression should strengthen both

The visual identity, voice and motion should help me perceive, act, understand and trust. When brand expression competes with those outcomes, the expression has become self-defeating.

## When trade-offs are necessary

I expect the brand to favour:

1. successful completion over spectacle;
2. informed choice over conversion;
3. clarity over cleverness;
4. control over forced engagement;
5. recovery over blame;
6. reliability over novelty; and
7. genuine inclusion over the appearance of inclusion.

---

# The brand promise I experience

Marketing may tell me that a brand is open, empowering, clear, dependable and human. The product proves or disproves each claim.

From my sighted perspective, a brand earns integrity when its visual expression and functional behaviour tell the same story:

- **I feel seen** because the meaning is perceptible and the identity does not obscure it.
- **I feel capable** because I can act with control and recover from mistakes.
- **I feel confident** because the language, sequence and consequences make sense.
- **I feel safe** because the experience remains dependable when conditions change.
- **I feel valued** because all four outcomes are delivered with care and respect.

That is the difference between brand presentation and brand experience. Presentation tells me what the organisation wants to mean. Experience shows me what it actually means.
