# Don’t Make Me Construct the Interface

## User expectations, needs, preferences and outcomes from a congenitally blind perspective

I have never seen a web page. I do not encounter a service as a picture, a collection of boxes or a surface arranged from left to right and top to bottom. I encounter it through speech, Braille, touch, keyboard commands and a sequence of objects that have names, purposes, states and relationships.

I can understand space, direction, shape, quantity and relationships. Congenital blindness does not prevent spatial thought. But I do not share the visual memory on which many digital conventions depend. Telling me that something is in the top-right corner, represented by a magnifying glass or plotted against an X and Y axis asks me to translate a visual convention before I can understand the information or act on it.

The problem is not simply that I cannot see the design. The problem is that the service may leave me to reconstruct its purpose, organisation and behaviour from a stream of fragments.

My central expectation is therefore:

> Do not make me construct the interface before I can use it.

This is not a list in which every expectation has equal weight. If choices have to be made, my ability to complete the task independently comes before convenience. Understanding and control come before speed. Speed comes before personal preference. A small inconvenience is not comparable with being prevented from acting, losing work or being unable to recover.

## How to compare my needs

### First: protect the outcome

These needs determine whether I can complete the task at all, complete it correctly and do so without surrendering my privacy or independence. Failure here is exclusion, not inconvenience.

### Second: give me understanding and control

These needs determine whether I know where I am, what I can do, what has changed and how to recover. Failure here makes an experience unreliable and may cause serious mistakes.

### Third: reduce interaction cost

These needs determine how much time, memory, repetition and concentration the service demands. Failure here may not block me immediately, but cumulative effort can make a nominally usable service practically unusable.

### Fourth: preserve richness and choice

These needs determine whether I receive comparable meaning rather than a thin substitute. Failure here leaves me with a poorer understanding or fewer choices than another person.

### Fifth: respect preference

These needs determine whether I can use the technologies, input methods, speech settings and working strategies that suit me. Preferences matter, but a preferred method should not be mistaken for the only method blind people use.

# 1. Outcomes I must be able to achieve

## I must be able to complete the same core task

I expect to buy the product, apply for the job, manage my money, book the appointment, read the article, give consent, refuse consent or contact support through the same service as everyone else.

An “assistive version,” special toolbar or separate simplified service is not an equivalent outcome when it contains fewer functions, becomes out of date or requires me to identify myself as disabled before I can proceed. Separate and add-on experiences can also conflict with the tools I already use. The better outcome is for the main service to work predictably with my chosen technology. Research and practitioner experience repeatedly show that unfamiliar add-ons are often ignored and may make the experience worse rather than better.

## I must be able to act independently

I should not need to ask a sighted person what an unlabelled control does, where an error appeared, which item is selected or whether a payment succeeded.

Independence and autonomy include privacy. If I have to ask someone else to read a medical result, bank balance, private message or identity document because the service withholds its meaning from my technology, the failure is more serious than a usability inconvenience.

## I must be able to make an informed decision

I need important conditions, costs, risks and consequences before I act. Information that is visually prominent but encountered after the action in my reading sequence arrives too late.

If a warning affects my decision, I expect to hear or feel it before the relevant choice. I should not have to discover it by reading backwards after committing myself.

## I must know whether the outcome occurred

After I submit, save, buy, delete, send or change something, I need an unambiguous result:

- what happened;
- whether it succeeded;
- what did not succeed;
- what remains to be done; and
- what I can do next.

A change in colour, position, animation or icon is not a result I can perceive. Silence is not confirmation.

## I must be able to recover

I expect mistakes to be reversible where possible. If reversal is impossible, I need a clear warning before the action.

When something goes wrong, I need to know what happened, which part needs attention, how to reach it and how to correct it without starting again. Recovery matters more than an elegant success path because the cost of becoming lost is much higher when I cannot inspect the whole interface at once.

## My work must not disappear

If I move away temporarily, open help, check a detail or return from another item, I expect my entries, selections and position to remain. Reconstructing a long form or relocating an item in a changing sequence is costly.

Losing my position in an endlessly loading list is particularly disruptive. A bounded set, a clear “load more” decision, a result count and a reliable return point give me a much stronger outcome than an unmarked stream.

# 2. Understanding and control

## I need the purpose before the detail

When I enter a service, I expect its name, the purpose of the current content and the main action to become clear quickly.

I do not want to listen through branding, promotions and repeated navigation before I can discover why I am here. The first useful information should let me decide whether I am in the right place.

## I need an overview that does not depend on seeing the whole

A sighted person may obtain an overview in a glance. I build one by moving among meaningful signposts: the service name, content title, section names, major actions, search, status and supporting information.

I need enough signposts to form a reliable mental model, but not so many that they become noise. More structure is not automatically better. A few accurate distinctions are more valuable than many weak or repetitive ones.

Screen readers provide ways to move between types of content rather than hearing every word in sequence. That strategy only works when the distinctions genuinely reflect the content.

## I need a stable and meaningful sequence

I encounter information one part at a time. I need each part to make sense in relation to what came before and what follows.

The most important order is not necessarily the visual order. It is the order in which I need information to understand and act:

1. context;
2. essential information;
3. choices;
4. consequences;
5. action; and
6. result.

When unrelated content interrupts that sequence, I have to hold more information in memory. When related information is separated, I may never discover the relationship.

Sequential access is not the same as passively listening from beginning to end. I use shortcuts, search and element lists to move directly. A well-designed sequence supports both careful reading and rapid movement. Research with mobile screen-reader users confirms that forced sequence, weak focus movement and poor labels greatly increase interaction cost.

## I need every object to identify itself

When I reach something I can act on, I expect to know:

- what it is;
- what it does;
- what it relates to;
- its current state; and
- what kind of response it expects from me.

“More,” “Click here,” “Edit,” “Open” and “Learn more” may be understandable when surrounded by visible context. Heard in a list of controls, they are often meaningless. I need the distinguishing information first: “Edit delivery address,” “More payment options” or “Read more about cancellation charges.”

I also expect familiar objects to behave in familiar ways. If something is announced as a button, I expect button behaviour. If it is announced as a link, I expect it to take me somewhere. A false identity is worse than no identity because it gives me instructions that do not work.

## I need state, not appearance

I need to know whether something is selected, checked, expanded, collapsed, unavailable, required, playing, muted, busy or complete.

I also need to know when that state changes. I should not have to leave the object, search the surrounding content and infer whether my action worked.

State information should be concise. Repeating a long label and description after every small change can obscure the one fact I need.

## I need my point of attention to move with the interaction

When I open a dialogue, menu, message or new step, I expect my point of attention to move into it. When I close it, I expect to return to the object that opened it or to the next logical place.

If new content appears while my attention remains behind it, the service feels as though nothing happened. If my attention is moved without explanation, I lose my place.

## I need changes to announce themselves in proportion to their importance

An urgent error may need to interrupt me. A background update usually should not.

I need to know about changes that affect my decision or task, but I do not want every changing number, loading message or promotional update spoken over what I am doing. Notification is valuable only when its timing and urgency match the consequence.

## I need interaction modes to remain predictable

The same keys or gestures may navigate content in one moment and enter text or operate a control in another. My technology often manages these shifts automatically, but unfamiliar custom interactions can make the current mode uncertain.

I expect an interaction to tell me enough about itself that I know whether I am reading, typing, selecting or operating a specialised control. I should not need to guess whether a keystroke will move me, enter a character or change a value. Léonie Watson’s explanation of browse, focus and application interaction shows why conventional behaviour is so valuable: custom controls can force two different interaction paradigms into the same experience ([Tink](https://tink.uk/understanding-screen-reader-interaction-modes/)).

## I need explicit boundaries

I need to know when I enter or leave navigation, a form, a dialogue, a group of options, search results or supporting information.

Boundaries reduce memory demand. They tell me which information belongs together and when I have exhausted a set. A boundary that can only be inferred from whitespace, a border or a change in background does not exist in my experience.

# 3. Reducing interaction cost

## I need to bypass repetition

Repeated navigation, promotional material and legal boilerplate can turn every visit into an endurance test. I expect a quick route to the primary content, the current task and other frequently used destinations.

The longer the repeated sequence, the more valuable bypass becomes. Removing three repeated items is a convenience. Removing fifty may determine whether the service is practical to use.

## I need direct routes, not compulsory tours

I value search, meaningful filtering, shortcuts and lists of major objects because they let me go directly to what I need.

Forced exploration is expensive. It makes me encounter every preceding item before reaching the one I want. Direct access gives me autonomy over the order in which I investigate the service.

## I need finite, countable sets

When possible, tell me how many results, options, steps or messages exist and which one I am on.

“Item 4 of 18” gives me a usable model. An unbounded stream gives me no sense of progress, completeness or the cost of continuing.

If more content can be loaded, I prefer to choose when that happens. I want the number already available, the number remaining where known, and my previous position preserved when I return.

## I need important words first

I often decide quickly whether to continue listening. Put the information that distinguishes one option from another near the beginning.

“Search products” is more useful than a long promotional phrase ending in “search field.” “Cancellation charge: £25” is more useful than a paragraph that reveals the charge at the end.

Important words first does not mean reducing everything to fragments. It means making early information carry useful meaning.

## I need concise speech without missing context

Every extra word has a time cost, particularly when repeated across many controls. I do not need decorative detail, duplicated visible text or the type of object repeated inside its name when my technology already announces it.

But brevity can become ambiguity. “Close” may be too vague when several layers are open; “Close payment options” gives me enough context. The goal is the shortest wording that remains unambiguous when heard by itself.

The BBC’s screen-reader UX guidance makes the same distinction: the nonvisual experience should be no more verbose than necessary, but controls may need context when they are encountered independently ([BBC](https://bbc.github.io/accessibility-news-and-you/guides/screen-reader-ux.html)).

## I need forms to minimise entry and correction

Text entry, especially on a touchscreen, can carry a high interaction cost. I expect the service to avoid asking for information it already has, accept common input variations and let me review before committing.

I may dictate, use a physical keyboard, type in Braille or use an on-screen keyboard. Dictation can be faster but creates errors that are difficult to locate and correct. A physical keyboard or Braille display may offer greater precision but may not be available. The service should not assume one input method.

When an error occurs, do not erase correct entries. Take me to the problem, explain it in ordinary language and preserve everything else.

## I need waiting to have a shape

If a process takes time, tell me that it has begun, whether it is still working and when it is complete. Where useful, give me an estimate or progress measure.

Repeated “loading” announcements are noise. Silence during a long process leaves me uncertain. I need enough feedback to distinguish waiting from failure.

# 4. Meaning, purpose and intent without dependence on visual memory

## I need the meaning of an image, not an inventory of pixels

I usually need to know why an image is present and what difference it makes to my understanding or decision.

For a functional image, tell me the action: “Search,” not “magnifying glass.” For an informative image, tell me the relevant information. For a decorative image, silence may be the best experience.

A description should be shaped by context. The same photograph may communicate identity in one setting, mood in another and nothing essential in a third. Good description communicates meaning rather than merely cataloguing what a sighted person sees.

## I need charts translated into questions and findings

An X axis and Y axis are conventions for arranging relationships visually. Describing their positions may tell me how the chart looks without telling me what it means.

Start with the question the chart answers. Then give me:

- the principal finding;
- the direction and scale of change;
- significant peaks, gaps or exceptions;
- the categories or time period involved; and
- access to exact values when I need to investigate further.

For example, “Complaints fell steadily from 420 in January to 180 in June, except for a temporary rise to 310 in April” is more useful than a description of a descending line.

If exact comparison is the purpose, I need the values in a form I can query or move through. If the trend is the purpose, a concise explanation may be better than requiring me to reconstruct the chart cell by cell.

## I need tables only when the relationships justify them

I can use a well-structured table. Blindness does not make row-and-column relationships unknowable. But a large matrix requires me to remember headings and relationships while hearing one cell at a time.

Use a table when comparing values across consistent categories is genuinely useful. Before the detail, tell me what the data represents and what matters. Keep the relationships stable, identify the relevant row and column as I move, and let me reach the underlying values without forcing me to hear the entire matrix.

If the task is to find one value, a question-and-answer structure or filtered result may be more efficient. If the task is to understand a trend, a summary may be more useful. Do not preserve a visual format when another form better serves the purpose.

## I need maps translated into destinations, routes and relationships

A map may combine location, distance, direction, terrain, boundaries, transport and points of interest in one visual field. A long description of that field is rarely an equivalent experience.

Start with my likely task:

- If I am travelling, give me ordered directions, decision points, distances, street crossings, transport changes and recognisable nonvisual landmarks.
- If I am choosing between places, give me comparable travel time, distance, facilities and relevant neighbourhood information.
- If I am learning an area, describe the major regions and how they connect before adding detail.
- If my location changes, tell me what changed and what the next relevant action is.

“Two hundred metres after the station entrance, turn left at the tactile crossing” gives me an actionable relationship. “The destination is above and to the right of the station” assumes a visual field and does not.


## I need comparison to be explicit

Visual designs often communicate comparison through alignment, proximity, colour, size or position. I need the comparison stated or made directly navigable.

Tell me:

- what is being compared;
- which attributes differ;
- which choice is selected or recommended and why;
- what stays the same; and
- what consequence each difference has.

Do not make me remember three long product descriptions and mentally subtract them from one another. Let me compare the attributes that matter.

## I need media to preserve narrative information

If action, identity, setting, text or a change in mood is communicated only visually, I need that narrative information at the point where it matters.

The objective is not a constant account of everything visible. It is enough information to follow the story, understand the evidence, recognise who is acting and appreciate the intended meaning without overwhelming dialogue or sound.

# 5. Preferences that must remain choices

## I may listen, read Braille or use both

Speech is not the only nonvisual output. I may prefer refreshable Braille for spelling, punctuation, code, private information, detailed editing or quiet environments. I may use speech for speed and Braille for precision.

Do not assume that all blind people read Braille, own a Braille display or prefer audio. Braille equipment can be expensive and inconvenient to carry; speech may be more widely available but less precise for some tasks.

## I may use desktop and mobile differently

A physical keyboard gives me tactile reference points and many direct commands. A touchscreen offers portability but fewer gestures and less physical orientation. I may choose a computer for complex work and a phone for short tasks.

The same outcome should remain possible on both, but the most efficient interaction may differ.

## I may adjust speed, punctuation and detail

An experienced listener may use speech at a speed that sounds unintelligible to a novice. I may change how much punctuation, object information and formatting is announced.

The service should communicate meaning without relying on one speech rate or verbosity setting. Punctuation should clarify the sentence, not carry essential meaning by itself.

## I may prefer shortcuts after learning the service

On first use, I need discoverability and explanation. On repeated use, I value direct commands, remembered choices and efficient routes.

Do not force expert interaction on a new user, but do not trap an experienced user in a tutorial-like sequence forever.

## I may refuse automation

Automatic selection, navigation, playback, completion or personalisation may appear helpful but can remove my control. I need to know what the service is doing on my behalf and be able to stop, review or reverse it.

Control is usually more valuable than surprise efficiency.

# 6. The comparisons that should guide decisions

When two ideas compete, I generally need the service to favour:

1. **Completion over presentation.** Let me achieve the outcome before polishing how the interface appears.
2. **Independence over assisted workarounds.** Do not make another person part of my interaction unless I choose that support.
3. **Meaning over visual fidelity.** Translate the purpose of a chart, map, image or layout instead of describing its appearance alone.
4. **Control over automation.** Let me initiate, pause, review and reverse consequential actions.
5. **Context before choice.** Give me what I need to decide before presenting the action.
6. **Stable structure over novelty.** Familiar behaviour reduces learning and prevents mode errors.
7. **Direct access over forced sequence.** Let me search, skip, filter and move by meaningful signposts.
8. **Finite progress over endless supply.** Counts, steps and boundaries help me judge effort and completeness.
9. **Recovery over blame.** Preserve my work and help me correct the problem.
10. **Concise distinction over repeated explanation.** Tell me what makes an item different, without drowning that difference in speech.
11. **The main experience over a separate “blind” experience.** I want the same current service, not a neglected substitute.
12. **Observed use over sighted simulation.** A sighted person temporarily turning on a screen reader can find obvious defects, but cannot reproduce my experience, strategies or fluency. Design decisions should be informed by people who use these technologies in daily life.

## The outcome I want

I do not need a visual interface translated word for word. I need a deliberately designed nonvisual experience that gives me comparable knowledge, agency, privacy and opportunity.

I should not have to imagine what the screen looks like in order to operate it. I should not have to remember a hidden two-dimensional arrangement. I should not have to infer that something changed because a sighted designer assumed the change was obvious.

I need the service to tell me, through its structure and behaviour:

- where I am;
- what this is;
- what matters;
- what I can do;
- what each choice will mean;
- what has changed;
- whether my action worked; and
- how to continue or recover.

When it does that well, I am not using a substitute for the interface. I am using the interface.
