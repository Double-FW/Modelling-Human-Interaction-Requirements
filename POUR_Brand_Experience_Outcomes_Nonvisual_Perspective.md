# POUR Reimagined as Brand Experience Outcomes

## A first-person nonvisual perspective for digital brands, products and services

I have never seen a web page, app screen, logo, typeface or colour palette. I do not encounter a digital brand as a visual composition. I encounter it through words, speech, Braille, sounds, haptics, interaction order, object names, feedback and the way the service responds to what I do.

This does not mean that branding disappears. It means the brand reaches me through different evidence.

I experience whether the organisation speaks clearly, whether its controls tell the truth, whether I can move efficiently, whether my choices are respected and whether the service remains dependable. These behaviours create tone and character as surely as colour and typography do for a sighted person.

The POUR principles can therefore be understood as four expected nonvisual brand experience outcomes:

- **Perceivable becomes: I feel seen.**
- **Operable becomes: I feel capable.**
- **Understandable becomes: I feel confident.**
- **Robust becomes: I feel safe.**

The functional and emotional outcomes cannot be separated. The functional experience gives me access to meaning and action. The emotional experience is what the pattern of those interactions teaches me about my place in the brand’s world.

My central expectation is:

> Do not make the brand disappear when the screen is no longer visual.

I do not speak for every blind person. People use speech, Braille, touch, physical keyboards, voice input and other methods in different combinations. This first-person account is one deliberately nonvisual way of examining the relationship between brand and experience.

## How I compare the outcomes

The outcomes are connected, but they are not equal in consequence.

1. **I need access to the meaning.** If I receive only fragments, the brand has not included me in the conversation.
2. **I need independent action.** Information without control leaves me dependent on someone else.
3. **I need context and consequence.** An action I can perform but cannot understand is not meaningful agency.
4. **I need continuing reliability.** An experience that works once, with one tool or only on the ideal path cannot support trust.

**Feeling valued** sits across all four. I do not feel valued because a statement says I belong. I feel valued when the product behaves as though my time, privacy, choices and experience were intentionally considered.

# 1. Perceivable: I feel seen

## My functional expectation

I expect the brand’s meaning, information, identity and choices to reach me without requiring sight or visual memory.

I do not need every visual property translated into words. I need to know what the visual material contributes:

- What does the image tell me?
- What mood or character is being established?
- Who is represented?
- What information is communicated through colour, shape or position?
- Is the visual object also a control?
- What changes when the visual state changes?

A description of appearance without purpose can leave me with detail but not meaning. “Blue circle containing a white arrow” tells me what a control looks like. “Continue to payment” tells me what I need to know.

Brand imagery may communicate identity, aspiration, humour, diversity or emotional tone. If all of that disappears from my experience, I receive the function but not the brand. If every visual is described exhaustively, I receive so much detail that the purpose disappears. The right outcome is comparable meaning with proportionate detail.

## My emotional expectation

I feel seen when the brand includes me in the intended experience rather than giving me the residue left after visual design is complete.

I feel unseen when:

- a campaign’s meaning is trapped inside an image;
- a famous personality or represented community is not identified;
- visual humour becomes unexplained silence;
- colour or position carries a status I am expected to infer;
- the brand’s tone disappears into generic technical labels; or
- I have to ask a sighted person what everyone else was shown.

Being seen does not mean constantly being told that the brand considered blindness. It means receiving the information, character and opportunity without being made exceptional.

## The brand outcome

The experience says:

> You are part of this audience. The meaning and character of the brand are intended for you too.

## Evidence I would encounter

- The service identifies itself clearly when I arrive.
- Meaningful imagery is translated by purpose and context.
- Decorative visual material does not create verbal noise.
- Names and descriptions preserve the brand’s tone without obscuring function.
- Visual states and changes become explicit information.
- I receive the same important distinctions and choices as other users.

# 2. Operable: I feel capable

## My functional expectation

I expect to move through the service, discover available actions, choose, enter information, review, submit, stop and recover using my preferred interaction method.

I encounter content in a sequence, but I should not be forced to hear everything in that sequence. I need meaningful signposts, search, shortcuts and direct routes to important actions.

Every object I can use should tell me:

- what it is;
- what it does;
- what it relates to;
- its current state; and
- how its state changed after I acted.

If I open a dialogue, menu or new step, I expect my point of attention to move into it. When I close it, I expect to return to a logical place. If something appears visually while my attention remains elsewhere, the interaction has happened without me.

I also expect the brand to respect the tools I already use. I should not have to activate a special “blind mode,” navigate an unfamiliar accessibility toolbar or accept a simplified version with fewer functions.

## My emotional expectation

I feel capable when the service responds to my intention rather than making me solve its interface.

I feel incapable when:

- controls have vague or missing names;
- the reading sequence separates information from its action;
- a custom interaction changes familiar commands without explanation;
- focus becomes lost after a change;
- repeated content makes a short task exhausting; or
- I can begin a journey but cannot complete it independently.

These failures do more than slow me down. They can make the brand’s language of empowerment feel dishonest.

## The brand outcome

The experience says:

> You can act here independently. Your way of interacting is part of the intended service.

## Evidence I would encounter

- I can reach every necessary action.
- Controls have specific names and predictable behaviour.
- I can bypass repetition and move by meaningful structure.
- My attention follows the interaction.
- I can stop, change my mind and recover.
- The service does not reserve full functionality for a visual method.

---

# 3. Understandable: I feel confident

## My functional expectation

I expect the service to give me enough context to form a reliable mental model.

I do not experience the whole interface at once. I build understanding from a sequence of titles, sections, controls, states and messages. The order in which I encounter them therefore shapes the meaning.

I expect:

- the purpose before the detail;
- the warning before the consequential action;
- the label with the control it explains;
- the result after the action;
- the error with a route to correction; and
- consistent language for the same concept.

Brand voice matters here. A playful label may sound distinctive but become meaningless when heard outside its visual context. A button called “Let’s go” may leave me asking where it goes. A control called “More” may be one of many identical objects in a list.

The brand can remain human and recognisable while naming actions specifically. Clarity does not require a cold voice.

## My emotional expectation

I feel confident when I can predict, verify and recover.

I know where I am, what is expected, what will happen and whether it happened. If the brand is uncertain, it tells me. If I make an error, it helps me correct it without implying that I am the problem.

I lose confidence when I hear unrelated fragments, when important information arrives too late or when an action produces silence. Because I cannot glance across the screen to resolve ambiguity, a vague label or unexplained change can destabilise my whole mental model.

## The brand outcome

The experience says:

> You have the context needed to decide. We will not make you guess what the interface means.

## Evidence I would encounter

- The service and current step identify themselves clearly.
- Sections reflect meaningful differences rather than visual decoration.
- Important words come early in names and messages.
- Choices make sense when encountered independently.
- Consequences arrive before commitment.
- Errors explain both the problem and the next action.
- Confirmation states what was completed, saved, sent or changed.

---

# 4. Robust: I feel safe

## My functional expectation

I expect the experience to keep working with my chosen combination of device, browser, screen reader, Braille display, keyboard, touch or voice input.

Robustness is not achieved merely because words are spoken. The relationships, states and behaviours need to survive changes in technology and updates to the product.

I expect:

- established objects to behave consistently;
- names and state information to remain accurate;
- my reading position and entered data to survive foreseeable interruption;
- updates to preserve learned concepts wherever possible;
- failure to produce a clear recovery route; and
- the main service to remain the maintained source of truth.

A brand that relies on an automated overlay or separate version may create the appearance of care while making my experience more fragile. I need the product itself to be dependable.

## My emotional expectation

I feel safe when I can trust the service beyond the ideal demonstration.

I can use it privately. I can return to it. I can update my technology without expecting the journey to collapse. I can make a mistake without losing everything. I know whether a result has been saved or sent.

I feel unsafe when an update silently changes the interaction, when my work disappears, when a screen-reader announcement contradicts the actual state or when I must ask another person to verify a private outcome.

## The brand outcome

The experience says:

> You can rely on us. The service will hold together across tools, time and ordinary disruption.

## Evidence I would encounter

- The product works through more than one reasonable interaction route.
- The experience remains coherent when I use speech, Braille or keyboard commands.
- Updates do not casually destroy my learned model.
- Failure and timeout preserve as much progress as possible.
- I can verify sensitive and consequential results independently.
- Recovery is part of the brand experience, not a technical afterthought.

# Feeling valued: the combined brand outcome

Feeling valued is not a fifth technical principle. It is the emotional verdict produced by all four outcomes.

I feel valued when the organisation has designed the nonvisual brand experience rather than merely allowing my technology to extract some text.

Functionally, that means:

- I receive comparable meaning and choice;
- I can complete the same core task;
- I can understand and verify the result;
- I can protect my privacy and independence; and
- I am not redirected to a reduced experience.

Emotionally, it means:

- I am an intended customer, colleague or citizen;
- the brand’s personality includes me;
- my time is not treated as infinitely available;
- my way of interacting is not framed as a problem;
- I can participate without announcing or explaining my disability; and
- the service’s claims about inclusion survive contact with the product.

A diverse campaign image may communicate belonging to a sighted audience. If the people are not identified in the description, that representation disappears for me. The brand may have performed inclusion visually while withholding it nonvisually.

# Balancing functional and emotional experience

## Function is the evidence of the promise

The brand cannot talk me into feeling seen when meaningful content is silent. It cannot call me capable while an unnamed control blocks the task. It cannot reassure me that I am safe while I need another person to confirm whether a payment succeeded.

Functional experience is how the emotional claim becomes credible.

## Emotional quality is part of the function

Tone, timing and dignity affect whether I can use the service confidently. A technically accurate error delivered without context may still leave me unable to recover. A verbose description may contain all the information while making the essential point difficult to find.

The functional and emotional are therefore not opposites. A respectful emotional experience often depends on precise functional decisions.

## Nonvisual brand expression needs design intent

The visual identity cannot simply be read aloud. The brand needs to decide how its character appears through:

- language and voice;
- the order and rhythm of information;
- the names of actions;
- the timing of feedback;
- sound and haptic cues where useful;
- the handling of errors and uncertainty; and
- the amount of detail provided in each context.

## When trade-offs are necessary

I expect the brand to favour:

1. independent completion over decorative equivalence;
2. meaning over descriptions of appearance;
3. specific names over clever ambiguity;
4. direct access over forced sequence;
5. proportionate feedback over silence or interruption;
6. stable behaviour over novelty;
7. privacy over assisted workarounds; and
8. the main experience over a separate “accessible” version.

# The brand promise I experience

I may never see the brand’s visual identity, but I experience whether its values are real.

From my nonvisual perspective, brand integrity means:

- **I feel seen** because the meaning, character and representation reach me.
- **I feel capable** because I can act independently and remain in control.
- **I feel confident** because context, sequence and consequence make sense.
- **I feel safe** because the service remains reliable across tools, time and disruption.
- **I feel valued** because the nonvisual experience was designed, not left to chance.

Marketing tells me what the organisation wants me to believe. Interaction gives me the evidence.

If the campaign says “everyone” but the product speaks in unnamed buttons, unexplained images and silent changes, the product wins the argument. If the brand claims empowerment but makes me ask for help, the interaction defines the brand more accurately than the slogan.

The nonvisual experience is not an alternative expression of the brand. It is the brand, encountered without sight.
