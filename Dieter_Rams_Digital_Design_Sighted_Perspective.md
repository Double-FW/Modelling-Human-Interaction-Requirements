# Dieter Rams’s Ten Principles Reimagined for Digital Products and Services

## A first-person sighted perspective

Dieter Rams developed his ten principles while thinking about physical products. I am applying their underlying questions to websites, apps and digital services.

I am sighted. I encounter an interface partly through colour, shape, scale, spacing, position, imagery and movement. I can often take in several elements at once and use their visual relationships to decide what deserves my attention. That is only one way of experiencing a digital product, but it strongly influences what design means to me.

I do not treat the ten principles as equal. For a digital service, usefulness, understanding and honesty are more important than novelty or visual distinction. Thoroughness, longevity and environmental responsibility protect the experience over time. Restraint and aesthetic quality support those outcomes; they should never be used to excuse missing information, weak controls or manipulation.

My overall test is simple:

> Does the design help me understand, decide and act—or does it merely ask me to admire the interface?

## How I compare the principles

When principles conflict, I place them in this order:

1. **I must be able to achieve the intended outcome.**
2. **I must understand the choices, consequences and current state.**
3. **I must be treated honestly and remain in control.**
4. **The service should be dependable, maintainable and economical with resources.**
5. **The experience should feel coherent, calm and well made.**
6. **Innovation and visual distinction should earn their place.**

This order matters. A visually refined payment journey that obscures a recurring charge is bad design. A novel control that I cannot recognise is bad design. A minimal screen that removes the words I need is not “less, but better”; it is simply less.

---

# 1. Innovation must improve my experience

I value innovation when it removes a real limitation, gives me useful capability or makes a difficult task meaningfully easier.

I do not experience novelty as an automatic benefit. An unfamiliar gesture, animated navigation system or conversational interface may be technically impressive while making my task slower and less predictable. If I first have to learn the designer’s idea before I can perform an ordinary action, the innovation has charged me a learning cost.

I expect innovation to answer three questions:

- What can I now do that I could not do before?
- What effort, risk or delay has been removed?
- Is the improvement greater than the cost of learning the new interaction?

As a sighted user, I can sometimes infer how a novel interaction works from animation, position or resemblance. That can make experimental design feel easier to me than it is for someone who does not receive those visual cues. I should not mistake my ability to infer an interaction for proof that the interaction explains itself.

The outcome I want is progress without unnecessary relearning.

---

# 2. Usefulness is the primary test

I use a digital product to accomplish something. I may want to transfer money, understand a policy, plan a journey, speak to someone, create work or make a decision. The design is useful when it helps me achieve that purpose accurately, confidently and with proportionate effort.

Usefulness is not the number of features. A product with many functions can be less useful if the important ones are difficult to find or operate. A dashboard full of cards may appear capable while forcing me to search for the one fact I need.

I expect the service to make the primary purpose clear and the primary route practical. Secondary features should not compete equally for attention. Promotions should not displace the task I came to perform.

I judge usefulness by outcomes:

- Did I complete the task?
- Did I understand the result?
- Did I avoid preventable mistakes?
- Could I recover if something went wrong?
- Was the effort reasonable for the importance of the task?

The outcome I want is successful action, not feature exposure.

---

# 3. Aesthetic quality should support use

Because I am sighted, aesthetic quality affects how I feel about a service before I read much of it. Typography, colour, spacing, imagery, movement and composition can communicate care, confidence, urgency or calm.

But beauty in a digital product is not a layer placed on top of function. I experience it in the relationships between things:

- the hierarchy that tells me what matters;
- the spacing that shows what belongs together;
- the consistency that makes the product feel learnable;
- the restraint that lets content breathe; and
- the feedback that makes an action feel complete.

Visual polish becomes harmful when it conceals. Low-contrast text, controls that blend into their surroundings, animated transitions that delay me and icon-only actions may create a fashionable appearance while reducing usefulness.

I also resist the idea that aesthetic quality means minimalism. Density can be beautiful when it supports expert work. Rich colour can be beautiful when it carries meaning without becoming the only source of meaning. A long form can be well designed when it is divided into understandable stages.

The outcome I want is visual coherence that strengthens comprehension and trust.

---

# 4. The product should explain itself

I want to understand what the service is, what I can do and what will happen next without studying instructions.

As a sighted user, I draw meaning from visual hierarchy. I recognise headings, groups, selected states, buttons, fields and navigation partly from their appearance and position. Good design makes those signals consistent with the actual behaviour.

I become uncertain when:

- a control does not look interactive;
- several actions look equally important although their consequences differ;
- the same visual style represents different behaviours;
- an icon depends on insider knowledge;
- information appears far from the choice it explains; or
- an action changes the interface without showing me what changed.

Self-explanation is not the absence of words. The clearest design may include a specific label, an example, a short instruction or an explanation of consequences. Removing these things can make the screen look simpler while forcing me to think harder.

The outcome I want is confident prediction: I can tell what an action will do before I take it.

---

# 5. The interface should remain in proportion to my task

I want the product to support my purpose without constantly demanding that I notice the product itself.

Unobtrusive digital design does not compete with my content, interrupt me unnecessarily or turn every action into a performance. It does not animate routine transitions merely to display craftsmanship. It does not cover the information I am reading with prompts, invitations or requests for feedback.

Restraint also means respecting context. A banking service should not behave like a game. A medical result should not be surrounded by marketing. A work tool should allow sustained attention.

However, unobtrusive does not mean invisible. Important actions, warnings and status changes need sufficient prominence. A designer can become so restrained that I cannot tell whether anything is interactive or whether my action succeeded.

The outcome I want is an interface that is quiet until it needs to speak clearly.

---

# 6. The service should tell me the truth

Honesty is not only the factual accuracy of the content. It is also the relationship between what the interface appears to offer and what it actually does.

I expect the service to be candid about:

- price and recurring charges;
- the use of my data;
- the consequences of accepting or refusing;
- what is automated;
- what is generated or inferred;
- limitations and uncertainty;
- how difficult cancellation will be; and
- whether a recommendation benefits me, the provider or both.

Visual design can manipulate me without making a false statement. A bright primary button can push acceptance while refusal is hidden in muted text. A countdown can manufacture urgency. A preselected option can turn inattention into consent. A progress indicator can imply that I am almost finished when several stages remain.

Good design does not exploit the fact that I scan, follow emphasis and prefer the easiest path.

The outcome I want is informed choice rather than engineered compliance.

---

# 7. The product should remain dependable over time

Digital products change more easily than physical ones, which makes longevity more difficult rather than less important.

I invest in learning where things are, what words mean and how a workflow behaves. A redesign can destroy that knowledge overnight. Moving familiar actions, renaming established concepts or replacing clear controls with fashionable patterns charges me for the organisation’s desire to appear new.

Long-lasting design uses stable concepts and interaction patterns. It changes when evidence justifies change, not simply because a new visual style has become popular.

Longevity also includes:

- preserving links, records and exported data;
- keeping older devices and browsers useful for a reasonable period;
- allowing me to retrieve my work if the service closes;
- maintaining familiar language across updates; and
- avoiding dependencies that make the product fragile.

The outcome I want is improvement without the needless destruction of learned behaviour.

---

# 8. Care must reach every detail

I experience the quality of a service most clearly at its edges: the error message, empty state, loading delay, interrupted payment, expired session or unusual combination of choices.

The main journey may look immaculate while these details reveal that the design was never completed.

Thorough design anticipates:

- what happens before data arrives;
- what happens when there is no data;
- what happens when the connection fails;
- what happens when I make a mistake;
- what happens when content is unusually long;
- what happens when I return later; and
- what happens after the apparent end of the journey.

Visual consistency matters here, but accuracy matters more. Dates, totals, labels and states must agree across the service. A beautifully aligned total that is wrong is not a small defect.

The outcome I want is confidence that the service has considered my whole journey, including failure and recovery.

---

# 9. The design should account for its environmental cost

A digital product may appear immaterial, but it depends on devices, data centres, networks, batteries, manufacturing and disposal.

I expect the service to avoid waste that offers me little value:

- unnecessarily large images and video;
- constant background activity;
- automatic playback;
- excessive tracking and duplicated data;
- updates that make functioning devices obsolete;
- computationally expensive effects; and
- journeys that transfer or reload the same information repeatedly.

Environmental restraint can improve my experience directly. A lighter service usually starts faster, uses less data, consumes less battery and works more reliably on an older device or poor connection.

I also consider attention a resource. A service that deliberately keeps me scrolling, refreshing and consuming is not environmentally or psychologically neutral.

The outcome I want is useful digital activity without avoidable material, computational or attentional waste.

---

# 10. Use as little design as the task allows

“Less, but better” is easy to misunderstand in digital design.

Less design does not necessarily mean fewer words, fewer controls or more empty space. It means removing what does not help me understand, decide or act.

I welcome the removal of:

- duplicated information;
- competing calls to action;
- decorative motion;
- unnecessary stages;
- internal terminology;
- choices the service can safely infer; and
- features that exist only to increase engagement.

I do not welcome the removal of:

- useful labels;
- context;
- confirmation;
- recovery options;
- visible system status;
- meaningful comparison; or
- control over consequential decisions.

Simplicity is the result of disciplined prioritisation, not subtraction for its own sake.

The outcome I want is no more interface than I need—and no less information or control than the task demands.

---

# A sighted interpretation of good digital design

From my sighted perspective, Rams’s principles lead me towards a digital experience that is visually coherent but not visually dominant.

I want design that helps me see relationships, priorities, state and consequence. I want beauty to emerge from usefulness, precision and proportion. I want innovation to remove a real limitation, restraint to protect my attention and honesty to protect my agency.

The principles also warn me against an easy mistake: confusing what looks simple with what is simple to use. A clean screen can conceal complexity, and a visually dense screen can make a complex task understandable.

For me, good digital design is not the smallest number of visible elements. It is the clearest relationship between my purpose, the choices available and the outcome I am trying to achieve.
